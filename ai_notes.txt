1. WHAT IS A WORD EMBEDDING AND WHY DO WE NEED IT?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A word embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents a word's
meaning. For example, "ocean" might be represented as [0.21, -0.54, 0.83, ...]
— 100 numbers in our case (GloVe 100d). The key property is that words with
similar meanings end up with similar vectors. So "ocean" and "sea" will have
vectors that point in roughly the same direction, while "ocean" and "hammer"
will point in very different directions.

WHY WE NEED IT:

Q-learning works with numbers, not strings. You can't feed the word "OCEAN"
into a learning algorithm — you need to turn it into something mathematical.

We need embeddings in three places:

1. State representation — the board is 25 words. To give the Q-agent a
   "picture" of the board, you represent each word as its vector. Now the agent
   can see patterns like "my team has a cluster of nature words" or "there's a
   dangerous assassin word near my intended clue."

2. Finding candidate clues — instead of searching all 3 million English words,
   you use cosine similarity (basically the angle between two vectors) to find
   words whose meaning is close to your team words and far from opponent/assassin
   words. That's what candidate_clues() does — it narrows ~3M possibilities down
   to ~50 reasonable clues.

3. Reward shaping — when the human guesser picks a word, you can measure how
   semantically close the clue was to what they guessed vs. what you intended,
   which helps produce a richer training signal than just "correct/wrong."

WHY GLOVE SPECIFICALLY:

GloVe was trained on billions of words of text, so it captures the kinds of
loose, category-based associations humans use in Codenames naturally — "APPLE,
TREE, LEAF" all cluster together even though they aren't synonyms. That's
exactly the kind of reasoning a Codenames spymaster needs.

2. WALKTHROUGH OF embeddings.py
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

IMPORTS & MODEL CONSTANT (lines 11–19)
KeyedVectors is gensim's structure for pre-trained embeddings — essentially a
giant dictionary of word → vector. _model is a module-level variable so the
model is only loaded once per session (loading it takes a few seconds).

load_model() (lines 22–29)
Downloads GloVe from gensim's servers on first run, saves it to ~/gensim-data,
and returns it. Every subsequent call just returns the already-loaded _model
without re-loading.

get_embedding() (lines 32–36)
Looks up a single word and returns its 100-number vector, or None if the word
isn't in GloVe's vocabulary. This is what you'll call later when building the
state representation for Q-learning.

_cosine_sim() (lines 39–40)
Measures how similar two vectors are. Returns a value between -1 and 1 —
closer to 1 means the words are semantically similar. scipy's cosine_distance
returns the distance (0 = identical), so we subtract from 1 to get similarity.

candidate_clues() (lines 43–111) — the main function:

  Lines 68–77: splits board words into team_vecs (words you want to hint at)
  and avoid_vecs (opponent + neutral + assassin words you don't want to
  accidentally point to). Fetches their vectors.

  Lines 83–84: averages all team word vectors into one mean_team_vec, then
  asks gensim "give me the 300 words in the entire vocabulary closest to this
  average." This is how we narrow 400k+ words down to a manageable candidate
  list.

  Lines 86–108: for each candidate clue word:
    - Skip it if it's already on the board, or contains numbers/punctuation
    - Compute cosine similarity to every team word
    - covered = team words above the sim_threshold (0.3)
    - team_score = sum of similarities to covered words (higher = better)
    - avoid_penalty = sum of excess similarity to non-team words
    - Final score = team_score - avoid_penalty

  Lines 110–111: sort by score, return top 50.

3. DEEP DIVE — candidate_clues() LINES 86–108
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Concrete example: team words = ["OCEAN", "FISH", "WAVE"], avoid words =
["KNIFE", "BLOOD"]. Candidate clue = "sea".

LINE 94 — get the clue's vector
    clue_vec = model["sea"]
Fetches the 100-number vector for "sea".

LINE 96 — compare clue to every team word
    team_sims = [("ocean", 0.82), ("fish", 0.61), ("wave", 0.55)]
Computes cosine similarity between "sea" and each team word.

LINE 97 — decide which team words are "covered"
    covered = ["ocean", "fish", "wave"]   (all above threshold 0.3)
If a word scored below 0.3 the clue is too semantically distant — a human
guesser probably wouldn't connect them, so we don't count it.

LINES 99–100 — skip useless clues
If the clue doesn't connect to any team word above the threshold, discard it.

LINE 102 — team score
    team_score = 0.82 + 0.61 + 0.55 = 1.98
Only sums similarities for covered words. Rewards both breadth (covering many
words) and depth (strong similarity).

LINES 103–106 — avoid penalty
    "sea" similarity to "KNIFE" = 0.45 → penalty = max(0, 0.45 - 0.2) = 0.25
    "sea" similarity to "BLOOD" = 0.15 → penalty = max(0, 0.15 - 0.2) = 0.0
    avoid_penalty = 0.25
The avoid_penalty_floor (0.2) means small incidental similarity is tolerated —
only excess similarity above 0.2 is penalized. Critical: without this the
agent might give a clue that leads the guesser to the assassin.

LINE 108 — final score
    ("sea", 1.98 - 0.25, ["ocean", "fish", "wave"])
    ("sea", 1.73, ["ocean", "fish", "wave"])
A clue that covers 3 words but is dangerously close to the assassin will score
lower than a clue that safely covers 2 words.

4. WHAT IS A Q-NETWORK?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

START WITH Q-LEARNING BASICS:
In regular Q-learning you have a Q-table — a giant lookup table where every
row is a state, every column is an action, and each cell holds one number: "how
much total reward do I expect if I take this action in this state?" After enough
experience the table fills in and the agent just picks the action with the
highest Q-value in any given state.

THE PROBLEM WITH A Q-TABLE FOR CODENAMES:
The state is a 200-dimensional float vector. The action is a 101-dimensional
float vector. You can't make a table with continuous, infinite-dimensional
inputs — there are infinitely many possible board configurations.

WHAT A Q-NETWORK DOES:
Instead of a table, you use a neural network as a function approximator. You're
asking it to learn the same thing the table would store —
Q(state, action) → expected reward — but as a continuous function over vectors
rather than a discrete lookup.

In our case:
    input:  301 numbers  (state + action concatenated)
              ↓
    layer 1: 128 neurons (learns low-level patterns)
              ↓  ReLU
    layer 2: 64 neurons  (learns higher-level patterns)
              ↓  ReLU
    output:  1 number    (predicted Q-value)

HOW IT TRAINS:
After each turn you have a real data point: you gave clue X in state S, and
the human guessed and you got reward R. The Bellman equation tells you what the
Q-value should have been:

    target = R + γ * max_Q(next_state)

The network predicted some value. The target is what it should have predicted.
You compute the difference (MSE loss), backpropagate the error through all three
layers, and nudge the weights slightly in the direction that reduces the error.

WHY TWO NETWORKS (q_net and target_net):
When you train, you're using the network's own predictions to compute the
target — which creates a feedback loop that makes training unstable (the target
keeps moving as you update weights). The fix is a second "frozen" copy
(target_net) that you only sync every 100 steps. The frozen copy computes
stable targets while q_net is the one actually being trained.

5. HOW WILL TRAINING HAPPEN? IS THIS RLHF?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

NOT EXACTLY RLHF, but it borrows the same core idea.

WHAT RLHF ACTUALLY IS:
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) trains a reward model first —
humans rank outputs, that ranking trains a separate neural net to predict human
preference scores, and then RL optimizes the main model against that reward
model. It's used to align LLMs like ChatGPT.

WHAT WE'RE DOING:
We're doing direct RL with human interaction in two phases:

Phase 1 — Learning from human demonstrations:
A human spymaster plays normally. After each turn the board state → clue →
guesser outcomes are recorded. The agent watches and does a Q-learning update
based on what happened. It learns "in board states like this, clues like that
tend to produce good outcomes." This is called behavioral cloning + Q-learning.

Phase 2 — Self-play with human feedback:
The AI clicks "AI Suggest", proposes its own clue, and the human operative
guesses normally. After the turn ends, record_outcome() is called with the
actual guess results. The reward signal comes directly from game outcomes.
No human needs to rate or rank anything — the game rules are the reward function.

COMPARISON TABLE:

                    RLHF                    Our approach
Reward source:      Learned from rankings   Directly from game outcomes
Reward model:       Separate neural net     Not needed
Human role:         Rates/ranks outputs     Plays the game normally
Complexity:         High                    Low

THE HONEST LIMITATION:
The reward only measures game outcomes, not clue quality. A clue could be
semantically brilliant but the operative just happened to misunderstand it —
the agent would still penalize that clue. Over many games this averages out,
but the agent needs a decent number of games before Q-values become reliable.

6. HOW MANY ITERATIONS DO WE NEED?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Our state space is continuous and high-dimensional (200 floats). The Q-network
needs to generalize across board configurations it's never seen, not memorize
specific boards. That's actually good — we don't need to see every possible
board, just enough variety that the network learns meaningful patterns.

ROUGH ESTIMATES BY PHASE:

Phase 1 (demonstrations):
  - Each game = ~10 turns (5 per team)
  - Each turn = 1 training step after the guesser finishes
  - ~200–500 games before Q-values stop being mostly noise
  - That's 2,000–5,000 training steps — small by ML standards

Phase 2 (self-play):
  - Agent starts at epsilon = 1.0 (100% random), decays by 0.995 per turn
  - To reach epsilon = 0.1: 0.995^n = 0.1 → ~460 turns (~46 games) just to
    switch from exploring to exploiting
  - To learn good Q-values: ~500–1,000 more games

Rough total: 700–1,500 games before the agent plays noticeably better than
random.

WHAT MAKES THIS HARD TO PREDICT:
- Operative consistency — different humans interpreting clues differently makes
  the reward signal noisy
- Clue vocabulary coverage — GloVe must have seen board words in similar
  contexts; obscure words hurt
- Network capacity — our small net trains fast but has limited expressiveness

PRACTICAL ADVICE:
Don't wait for 1,000 games. After ~50 games clues should start looking less
random. After ~200 it should pick thematically coherent clues most of the time.
The save/load system means every game accumulates — you don't have to do 1,000
games in one sitting.

7. HOW LONG WOULD IT TAKE TO TRAIN?
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PER-TURN COST:
- candidate_clues() — calls model.most_similar() on a 400k-word vocabulary.
  ~0.5–2 seconds per turn depending on machine.
- train_step() — pure numpy on a tiny network with batch size 32. <10ms.
- record_outcome() — runs candidate_clues() once more for next state.
  Another 0.5–2 seconds.
Total: ~1–4 seconds of compute per turn, mostly embedding lookup.

PER-GAME COST:
- ~10 turns × 2–4 seconds = 20–40 seconds of compute per game
- But you also have to actually play the game: 10–20 minutes
The bottleneck is human time, not compute time.

TOTAL WALL-CLOCK TIME FOR 1,000 GAMES:

                    Low estimate    High estimate
Time per game:      10 min          20 min
Compute per game:   20 sec          40 sec
Total:              ~170 hours      ~340 hours

WHAT'S ACTUALLY FEASIBLE:

Games       Human time      What to expect
20–50       3–10 hours      Agent stops being random, clues become thematic
50–150      10–50 hours     Noticeably coherent clues, learns to avoid assassin
150+        50+ hours       Meaningful Q-value convergence

RECOMMENDATION:
For the project, frame it as a proof of concept with early-stage training —
show the learning curve (loss decreasing, epsilon decaying, reward per turn
increasing over games) rather than a fully trained agent. 20–30 games with
logged metrics is enough to demonstrate the system works and is learning.
That's a completely valid result for a course project.
